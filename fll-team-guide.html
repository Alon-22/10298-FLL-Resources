What is FIRST LEGO League?

FLL is a program for students in grades 4 through 8 built around one idea: learning by doing. Over the course of a season, you will engineer a robot, conduct real research, develop a solution to an actual problem, and present your work to judges. The skills you build: critical thinking, collaboration, public speaking, and creative problem solving. These are the same ones engineers, scientists, and leaders use every day. The competition is the deadline, not the point.

The 4 components judges score you on
Component 01

Robot Game

Your robot has 2.5 minutes on the game table, entirely on its own. You design it, build it, and program it to complete as many missions as possible. Points come from missions completed, not speed.

What you learn: mechanical design, programming, testing under pressure
Component 02

Robot Design

Judges sit down with your team and ask how you built it. They want to understand your decisions: what you tried, what failed, what you changed, and why. Every team member should be able to answer.

What you learn: engineering process, documentation, technical communication
Component 03

Innovation Project

You identify a real-world problem connected to the season theme, research it by talking to actual experts, design a solution, and present it to judges. Then you share it with people outside of FLL.

What you learn: research, problem definition, presentation, community engagement
Component 04

Core Values

Judges observe how your team works together throughout the day. They are looking for genuine collaboration, respect, and how you handle challenges as a group. This is not a performance. It is how you actually act.

What you learn: teamwork, leadership, gracious professionalism

At competition, each component is scored separately. A team can win a Robot Game award, a Project award, a Core Values award, or a Champion award for excelling across all four. The goal is not to be perfect at one thing. It is to show growth and depth across everything.

What a typical season looks like

August: Season Launch

The new challenge is revealed

FIRST releases the season theme and robot game rules. You'll find out what's on the game table and what kind of problem your project should focus on. Start a team journal right away.

August–September

Build, code, and pick your project topic

Start building your robot and testing early missions. At the same time, pick a specific problem to research for your project. Both happen at once, so don't wait on one until the other is done.

October–November

Refine and reach out

Work on your robot's consistency, contact experts for your project, and start rehearsing your Innovation Project presentation and Robot Design explanation for the judging session. Most qualifier competitions happen in November or December.

November–January

Compete

First comes a qualifier event. If you advance, there are regional, state, and potentially world championship events. The judging process is the same everywhere; the competition just gets bigger.

Things every new team should know

You don't need to be perfect. You need to be consistent.

Missions you can execute reliably every run are worth far more than missions you attempt and fail. Go through all the missions, cut the ones you cannot execute consistently, and attempt as many of the rest as you can. Judges want to see that you understood the full mission board and made deliberate decisions about what to include and cut.

All three areas are judged equally. Don't ignore any of them.

A lot of first-year teams put almost all their time into the robot and rush everything else at the end. Teams that score well keep all three areas moving throughout the whole season.

Documentation is as important as the work itself.

Judges can only score what they can actually see. A great robot with no photos or notes is worth less than a simpler one with a clear journal and photos from each step. Start on day one.

Gracious Professionalism is not optional. It's scored.

FLL has a concept called Gracious Professionalism. It means competing hard while being genuinely kind to other teams, judges, and volunteers. Judges watch how your team acts all day, not just inside the judging room.

Asking for help is fine. It actually helps your score.

Reaching out to experts, talking to other teams, and learning from mentors all count as evidence in judging. FLL wants teams to look for knowledge outside their own group.

Area 01

Robot Design

How you build your robot matters as much as what you build. This section covers the design process, engineering decisions, and how to document your work for judges.

The most important thing to understand about robot design

Big robot: the common mistake
  • Gets stuck in tight table spaces
  • Heavy and slow, hard to control precisely
  • Unbalanced weight causes inconsistent turns
  • More things to break during a run
  • Different results every time
Small, compact robot: what works
  • Fits anywhere on the table
  • Light and fast, navigates precisely
  • Stable and predictable run after run
  • Fewer moving parts = fewer failures
  • Same result nearly every time
Key lesson from experienced teams

Small, compact, and lightweight robots are almost always more consistent. A lot of teams spend multiple seasons figuring this out. Start small from the beginning.

Where to start: building your robot

1

Build your robot as small as possible

Aim for a compact, sturdy base about the size of a large paperback book. Don't add mission-specific attachments yet. Just get the robot driving straight and turning accurately before anything else.

2

Write code for one mission and test it 10 times in a row

Don't try to code everything at once. Pick one mission, write the code, and run it ten times. Count how many times it succeeds. If it's below 7 out of 10, fix the problem before moving on. Only move to the next mission once this one is reliable. This is how you build up to many missions without losing consistency.

3

Add missions one at a time, always checking consistency

Every time you add a new mission sequence, re-test your earlier missions too. Adding new code can sometimes break what was already working. Catching this early saves you from big surprises at competition.

Getting started Build and document First-year teams

Take notes after every practice run

Keep a notebook or shared doc. After each run, write down what worked, what failed, and what you're changing next. Judges will want to see this journal.

Keep attachments simple and light

For your first season, focus on using your robot's built-in motors and sensors before adding complex attachments. Simple solutions that work beat clever solutions that break.

Next level Engineering process Returning or advancing teams

Set written design goals before building

Decide your robot's maximum size, weight, and target consistency before you start building. Writing those goals down first helps your team make faster, better decisions.

Example: body under 5.5″ × 5″

Document your robot's evolution

Not every team needs a full V2 before their first event, and that's fine. What matters is intentional improvement: if you change something, record why, what you changed, and whether it worked. Even a single well-documented robot with logged iterations shows judges real engineering process. Only consider a redesign if the core structure is causing consistent failures that practice won't fix.

Build mission-specific attachments

One attachment per mission is more reliable than a multi-function arm. Keep them lightweight. Heavy attachments slow your robot and throw off its turning accuracy.

Research advanced coding techniques

Once your robot is solid at the basics, look into gyro-based turns, color sensor line-following, and PID control. These help a lot with accuracy as competition gets more serious.

PID = much more consistent navigation

  • ✓Do you have photos or a journal documenting how the robot evolved over the season?

  • ✓When something went wrong (it always does), can you explain the problem and how you fixed it?
  • ✓Does your code have any interesting or clever techniques worth explaining to judges?
Area 01

The Robot Game

Your robot gets 2.5 minutes per run to complete missions completely on its own. Most competitions give you three runs and count your best two. There are many missions on the board — most teams define a scope: a focused set of missions they can run reliably, rather than attempting everything and failing half of them.

Mission Path Planner

Map your robot's route for each run. Draw directly on the field, then export or print to show judges your strategy.

Numbered circles = generic mission areas. Each run gets its own color. Export as PNG to show judges on a tablet.

How the robot game works

The game table is a 4 by 8 foot mat with mission models built from LEGO pieces. Before each run, you place your robot at a launch pad, press start, and step back. You can't touch it again until it comes back to base. After the run a referee counts your score. Between runs you make changes in the pit area, then go again. Every team at every competition uses the same table.

How to pick your missions

1

Survey all missions first

Every FLL season has many missions — often 15 to 20 or more. Your team's job is to go through every single one and understand what it requires. Don't skip any — sometimes the ones that look hard are worth a lot of points for surprisingly little effort.

2

Attempt as many as you can execute reliably

The goal is not to do as few missions as possible — it's to attempt as many as your team can execute reliably. Cut the ones that are too risky or not worth the setup time, and go after the rest. Judges will ask why you skipped certain missions — having a real answer is part of your Robot Design score.

3

Plan your runs on paper before coding

Map out which missions go in each run, their point values, and your run order. Build in a backup plan for if a mission fails mid-run. Document which missions you cut and why — "we didn't get to it" is a weak answer. Knowing your full strategy on paper prevents panicking at the table.

Getting started Mission strategy basics First-year teams

Survey all missions — then cut strategically

FLL seasons typically have 15–20+ missions on the board. Go through every mission, figure out which ones to cut (too hard, too risky, not worth the points), and then go after the rest. Reliable execution beats an ambitious attempt that fails.

Know your expected score before you compete

For every mission you plan to attempt, write down its point value. Add them up and memorize your expected total. Walk into competition knowing exactly what you are going for. If judges ask your expected score and you cannot answer, that signals a lack of strategy.

First competition goal

Finish at least one mission in every single run. That's your first goal. A team that scores 20 points in all three runs shows more than a team that nails one run and fails the other two.

Next level Refine your run strategy Returning or advancing teams

Plan a full run strategy on paper

Before every practice session, map your robot's path for each run. Knowing your mission order, approximate timing, and decision points gives you something concrete to improve from session to session.

Track consistency percentage per mission

If you're finishing a mission less than 70% of the time, fix it or drop it. A mission you can't complete reliably will cost you more than it's worth.

Area 02

Innovation Project

Every FLL season has a theme. Your team needs to find a real problem connected to that theme, come up with a solution, talk to real experts, and share what you found with people outside FLL. Judges look at how deep your research went, how creative your solution is, and how many people you reached.

What makes a good Innovation Project?

A good project starts with a specific real problem backed by real data. Not just "pollution is bad," but something like: "According to the EPA, 40% of rivers in the US are too polluted to swim in." From there, your team comes up with a creative solution, builds or tests something, talks to real experts, and shares what you learned. Your project doesn't have to be a finished product. A well-researched idea with a rough prototype and useful expert feedback can score just as well.

The four phases of every Innovation Project

1

Identify a specific problem with real data

Don't start too broad. "Food waste is a problem" is too vague. "According to the USDA, 30 to 40% of the US food supply is wasted every year, costing consumers $161 billion" is a real problem statement. Find a number from a credible source like a government agency, major nonprofit, or university.

2

Design and build a solution

Your solution can be an app, a device, a service, or any creative idea. It doesn't have to fully work. A rough prototype that shows the concept is enough at most competition levels. Take photos of every step, even when things fail or look messy. That documentation is exactly what judges want to see.

3

Contact real experts and use their feedback

Reach out to people who know about your problem or could help with your solution. Email works fine, and most people are happy to talk to student teams. For your first season, aim to contact at least one expert. When you do, show how what they told you actually changed something in your project.

4

Share your project beyond your team

Share your project with people outside FLL. A class presentation, a community event, a local nonprofit, or a government official all count. The broader the reach, the better. Even a simple website anyone can visit counts.

Getting started First-year project essentials First-year teams

Find one credible statistic about your problem

A number from the CDC, USDA, EPA, WHO, or a major university study is enough to anchor your problem statement. One strong number beats a paragraph of vague claims.

Build something physical, even if rough

A rough model you can demonstrate is a lot more memorable than slides. It doesn't have to work perfectly. Just show that you built something.

Contact one expert before competition

Email someone who works in the area of your problem. Tell them you're a student team and ask a few specific questions. Even one real response makes a noticeable difference.

Present to at least one outside audience

A class presentation, a demo for family and neighbors, or even a short video posted online counts. You need evidence that you shared your project beyond your own team.

Next level Going deeper with research and outreach Returning or advancing teams

Contact three or more experts

Reach someone who has the problem, someone with technical expertise, and someone who could actually implement or fund your solution. Different perspectives from different sectors matter.

Show how feedback changed your design

Tell judges: "An expert told us X, so we changed Y." Documented iteration based on expert input is worth much more than listing experts you contacted but didn't act on.

Log problems and solutions during development

When your prototype breaks or your plan changes, write it down: what failed, what you tried, what worked. This log becomes your evidence of a real engineering process.

Create a project website or QR code

A simple project website makes sharing infinitely easier. You can send the link to every expert you contact, include a QR code in your portfolio, and demo it at competition.

Reach decision-makers, not just general contacts

Presenting to a city council member, a nonprofit director, or a school principal hits differently than emailing a general inbox. People who can actually do something with your idea are the contacts judges notice.

Test with real users from your target group

If your solution is for elderly people, have elderly people actually try it. Critical feedback from real users is some of the best evidence you can bring to a judging session.

How to contact an expert (and actually get a response)

Most experts are happy to help if you keep your ask short and clear. Keep your email under 150 words. Introduce your team and grade level, describe your problem in one sentence, and ask two or three specific questions. Don't ask them to solve your project for you; ask for their knowledge. If you don't hear back in a week, one follow-up is fine and often works.

  • ✓Who they are: a professor, nonprofit employee, government worker, local business owner, anyone with real knowledge of your problem
  • ✓Why you're asking: "We're a student FLL team researching [problem] and we'd love to hear from an expert in [their field]"
  • ✓What you're asking: two or three specific questions, not "can you help us with our project"
  • ✓Record their response and note exactly how it influenced your project

What judges look for in the Innovation Project

Judges are checking four things: Is the problem real and backed by data? Is the solution creative and workable? Did you actually talk to experts and change something based on what they said? And did you share this with real people outside your team? If you can answer all four with specific examples and documentation, you'll score well even if your prototype isn't perfect.

Area 03

Core Values

Core Values is not a separate judging session. It is observed by judges throughout your entire 30-minute judging block. While your team presents the Innovation Project and explains the Robot Design, judges are also watching how you treat each other, who speaks, whether you listen, and whether your teamwork looks genuine.

How does judging actually work?

Your team has one 30-minute judging session. It is structured as: a 2-minute welcome while you set up, a 5-minute Innovation Project presentation, 5 minutes of Innovation Project Q&A, a 5-minute Robot Design explanation, 5 minutes of Robot Design Q&A, and up to 8 minutes of Final Share where you can add anything and judges give verbal feedback. After you leave, judges spend 10 minutes scoring together. Core Values is not a separate event, judges are watching how your team works together throughout the entire session.

The six Core Values: what they actually mean

Discovery

Show what you actually learned this season

Not just "we learned a lot." Something specific. A comparison you did, a technique you figured out, a mistake you had to correct. The more concrete, the better.

Innovation

Show creative thinking in action

Did you try something new? Did you solve a problem in a way nobody expected? Walk judges through how you got there. They care about the thinking, not just the answer.

Impact

Show that your work reached real people

What real difference did your team make this season? This covers your Innovation Project, but also any mentoring, teaching, or outreach your team did on its own.

Inclusion

Make every member feel genuinely valued

Every person on your team should have a real contribution, not just a title. Write your own definition of what inclusion means for your specific team.

Teamwork

Work together, don't just divide tasks

Show that decisions were made together, that members helped each other, and that when you needed knowledge you didn't have, you went and got it. Asking for help is a strength in FLL.

Fun

Actually enjoy your season. And document it.

Fun is actually scored. You need specific evidence, not just a claim that your team gets along. Name a tradition, a moment, or an activity from this season that was genuinely fun.

Gracious Professionalism: the most important FLL concept

Compete hard. Be kind. That's the whole idea. Gracious Professionalism means treating competitors, judges, and volunteers with real respect, even when things go wrong. Cheer when other teams score. Share tips you didn't have to share. Never celebrate another team's failure. Judges watch this all day, not just during your judging session.

Getting started Core Values basics for first-year teams First-year teams

Every member should be able to speak

Judges ask individuals questions, not just the team as a whole. Make sure everyone is ready to explain their role and at least one thing they did this season. Nobody should be caught off guard.

Give everyone a clear role

Builder, programmer, project lead, presenter, note-taker. Even informal roles matter. When everyone has something to own, the whole team benefits and nobody is stuck with nothing to say.

Write down at least one "Discovery"

What's one specific thing your team learned this season that surprised you? Something tied to your robot, your project, or even how you work together. Give judges something real and specific to ask about.

Help another team at competition

If you see another team struggling with anything, help them. This is Gracious Professionalism in action. Do it because it's the right thing to do, and write it down for your portfolio.

First session tip

Practice answering this as a team: "Tell us about a challenge you faced this season and how you handled it." Have a real, specific story ready. Make sure more than one person takes part in telling it.

Next level Going deeper with Core Values documentation Returning or advancing teams

Document your conflict resolution process

When your team disagrees, how do you settle it? A vote? The mentor decides? Strongest argument wins? Write it down, and be ready to name a specific time it came up this season.

Write your team's Inclusion statement

What does Inclusion actually mean to your team, given your backgrounds and how you work together? Write it in your own words. One paragraph is enough. Don't copy it from the rulebook.

Mentor or teach someone outside your team

Even one session helping a newer team or younger students builds evidence for both Impact and Teamwork. Doing it more than once is a lot stronger than a single visit.

Plan some documented Fun activities

Regular game nights, a competition-day tradition, a specific thing only your team does. Documented, scheduled fun beats a vague claim that your team gets along.

Show how your values show up in all three areas

Did your Teamwork show up in how you built the robot? Did your Discovery connect to the project? At higher competition levels, judges look for teams where Core Values show up genuinely throughout the entire judging session, not just when they are directly asked about it.

Give each member a spotlight in your portfolio

Write one paragraph per member: who they are, how long they've been on the team, and what they specifically did this season. Judges should know everyone's name and contribution before the session even starts.

Hard questions aren't harder because the words are bigger. They're hard because generic answers fail them. Here's what makes a question hard:
They require a real story, not a concept
Easy: "What is Gracious Professionalism?" Hard: "Give an example of a team you saw today showing Gracious Professionalism." The hard version requires you to actually be paying attention at the event, not just knowing a definition.
They test whether kids did the work
Judges are trained to ask "walk me through your code" or "describe your coach's role" specifically to find out if the adults did the heavy lifting. If a kid can't explain their own robot's code, that's a red flag. Every member should understand the whole project at a basic level.
They ask about failure, not just success
Teams that say "everything went great" lose credibility. Judges look for: what broke, what you tried that failed, and what you learned. A team that has a real failure story told with confidence scores better than a team pretending everything was perfect.
They address quiet members directly
Judges may direct a question to a specific kid who hasn't spoken yet. Everyone on the team needs to be able to answer basic questions about the project and robot. Practice having your quieter members answer out loud, not just nod along.
They introduce new information mid-session
A judge might say: "We happen to know a similar product already exists" or "your expert mentioned that approach has a known problem." These are curveballs. The right move is to stay calm, acknowledge it, and explain what you would do next. Judges are not trying to trick you. They're testing your thinking.
They ask about trade-offs, not just achievements
Advanced teams can say "we chose reliability over maximum points" or "we didn't attempt that mission because the time cost was too high." Knowing what you decided NOT to do, and why, is a sign of strategic thinking that impresses judges more than a list of what you accomplished.
